Automation technician

Automation technicians find the repetitive jobs inside a business, such as invoices retyped or reports copied by hand, and build small systems that do them automatically. Demand is strong because every organisation has this kind of work and few people who can remove it. The role suits practical problem solvers who like seeing hours saved, and you do not need a computer science degree; many builds need no code at all. Honestly, it is screen heavy and the tools change often, but the results show up fast.

A day in this job

You begin by checking that yesterday's automations ran cleanly and fixing anything that stalled. Mid morning you sit with a colleague and map a task they repeat every week. The afternoon goes on building and testing the workflow that removes it. You finish by noting the hours it will save each month.

How AI is changing it

AI now builds much of an automation from a plain description of the task, so the technician's job moves up a level. You specify what should happen, test that it really does, and make sure it fails safely when something changes. Being trusted near live business systems stays human.

What you need to start

People enter with a range of qualifications or experience, commonly GCSEs and A levels plus a vocational qualification. Apprenticeships exist and some employers ask for a degree.

Typical pay: £750 a week (about £39,000 a year).

The steps, in order

  1. Learn automation basics Free starter courses on automation tools show how easy it is to begin.
  2. Automate something real Pick one repetitive task, yours or a friend's, and automate it from start to finish.
  3. Do a digital or automation bootcamp Funded bootcamps turn this skill into something you can prove, fast.
  4. Get an IT qualification A BCS qualification proves your skills to employers.
  5. Build a portfolio of builds Three working automations, with the time saved by each, tell the story.
  6. Apply for jobs Automation technician and junior automation jobs value builds you can show working.
