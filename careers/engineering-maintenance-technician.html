Engineering maintenance technician

Engineering maintenance technicians keep the machines in factories and plants running. It suits people who like practical problem solving, who stay calm when something breaks, and who enjoy knowing the whole site relies on them. Be honest about the hard parts: shifts, weekends and bank holidays are common, and the work can be noisy, dirty and sometimes outdoors. The reason to consider it now is that every plant needs its kit kept running, so the work is steady, and the skills move with you from one industry to the next.

A day in this job

Your shift might start with a list of routine servicing to plan and share out. Then a pump, a motor or a gearbox needs attention, or a control panel shows a warning that you have to track down. You find the fault, order the part, fix it and write up a job report so the production manager knows where things stand. Safety checks and risk assessments run through the whole day.

How AI is changing it

AI is changing how maintenance is planned. Sensors and software can spot the early signs of wear and suggest when a machine needs servicing, so fewer breakdowns come as a surprise. Reports and parts orders take less time too. The diagnosis on the floor, the repair and the call on whether a machine is safe to run stay with the technician.

What you need to start

You can start with no set qualifications on a Level 2 foundation apprenticeship, or as an engineering operative who trains on the job. A Level 3 engineering maintenance technician apprenticeship usually asks for about five GCSEs at grades 9 to 4, including English and maths. College courses and T Levels in maintenance or engineering also lead in.

Typical pay: £660 a week (about £34,300 a year).
