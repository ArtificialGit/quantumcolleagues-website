Trainee counsellor

Counselling is skilled listening: helping people make sense of grief, anxiety and stuck places by sitting with them, not fixing them. It rewards life experience, so many people come to it in their thirties, forties or later, training part time while they earn elsewhere. Be clear eyed: qualifying takes years, placements are often unpaid, and the work asks a lot of you emotionally. It repays with depth, real usefulness and steady demand from schools, charities, the NHS and private practice.

A day in this job

A counselling day is a rhythm of sessions, about an hour at a time, with space between to write notes and reset. In training, your week mixes classes, practice sessions and a placement, plus your own supervision. Some clients arrive in crisis, most arrive stuck. You end tired in a particular, worthwhile way.

How AI is changing it

Notes, scheduling and the admin around a practice are increasingly handled by AI tools, and theory is easier to revise with one beside you. What can never be delegated is the relationship: presence, deep listening and the trust built in the room.

What you need to start

There are no formal entry qualifications, but relevant experience is necessary, and most employers expect you to hold or be working towards a counselling diploma or professional accreditation. A DBS check is likely.

Typical pay: £440 a week (about £22,900 a year).

The steps, in order

  1. Explore the jobs Read about counsellor and youth worker jobs, including the training each one needs.
  2. Try a helpline or youth club Helplines, youth clubs and mentoring schemes train their volunteers. That training counts.
  3. Under 30? Try the King's Trust Its programmes build confidence and open doors into youth and community work.
  4. Take an introduction to counselling Level 2 counselling courses run at local colleges. They are often funded for adults.
  5. Qualify step by step Move on to Level 3 and 4 part time while you work. Most counsellors train this way.
  6. Find your setting Schools, charities, the NHS and private practice all need skilled, grounded people.
