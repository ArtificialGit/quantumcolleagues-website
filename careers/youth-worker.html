Youth worker

Youth work means walking alongside young people: clubs, mentoring, street based teams, being the reliable adult who does not flinch. Life experience genuinely matters more than a degree here, and volunteering is the recognised way in. Honestly, funding is patchy, evening and weekend hours are standard, and progress with a young person is rarely a straight line. But when a teenager who trusts nobody starts trusting you, you understand exactly why the work exists.

A day in this job

Afternoons and evenings are your busy time: setting up the youth club, running sessions, refereeing football, having the quiet word that matters. Daytime hours go on planning, funding forms and meetings with schools or social workers. Some sessions fizz, others flop. You keep showing up, because that is the whole method.

How AI is changing it

Session plans, funding bids and reports, the paperwork that eats youth work, are all getting faster with AI drafting. That means more face time with young people. The trust, the banter and the showing up week after week stay human.

What you need to start

You need a qualification accredited by the National Youth Agency at Level 2 or 3, and apprenticeships are available. Some employers ask you to be over 21, and a DBS check is likely.

Typical pay: £550 a week (about £28,600 a year).
Official UK pay for "Youth and community workers" (ONS ASHE 2022, via LMI for All). A guide, not a promise.

The steps, in order

  1. Explore youth work Read youth worker profiles to see the settings, from clubs to street based teams, and what each route asks for.
  2. Volunteer with young people Youth clubs, mentoring schemes and sports sessions train their volunteers, and that experience is the recognised first rung.
  3. Under 30? Try the King's Trust Its free programmes build confidence and open doors into youth and community work for people aged sixteen to thirty.
  4. Get the qualification You need a National Youth Agency accredited qualification at Level 2 or 3, and many courses are funded for adults.
  5. Apply widely Councils, charities, schools and housing associations all hire youth workers, so apply across sectors and ask about evening rotas.
  6. Grow once you're in Experienced youth workers move into senior practice, management or specialist work, with apprenticeships and funded study supporting the climb.
